# Fresh self-state, not delayed extrapolation, stabilizes delayed visual steering: modeling and tests across four public neural-behavioral datasets

## Abstract

Sensory feedback delays destabilize rapid self-motion control. In Drosophila steering, proposed solutions include efference-copy cancellation, all-or-none gating, and rapid mechanical feedback. Using a delayed flight-steering model, we show that the ingredient that computationally stabilizes control is **fresh current self-state** routed through a fast feedback path, with pathway gating making an independent model-level contribution. Neither trivial extrapolation nor a full forward-model observer built solely from delayed observations rescues stability, and matched-budget instantaneous classical control does not either. We then tested the empirical boundaries of these claims across four public neural-behavioral datasets, reporting every outcome regardless of direction. Fly-level reproduction of a counter-evidence dataset validated our pipeline and localized stationary-pattern suppression away from elementary motion detectors and measured LPTC; a behavioral model selection between graded and gated suppression was inconclusive at available sample sizes. In simultaneous HS membrane potential and wing-beat recordings, past motor history carried a small, consistent incremental predictive signal for neural activity (9/12 animals; p=0.0503, confidence interval crossing zero), reported as additional predictive information only. In a dataset with a genuine optogenetic intervention axis, we found that a naive state-dependent perturbation test is defeated by generic behavioral autocorrelation, and that a residual pipeline shrinks but does not eliminate the artifact at n=6, leaving perturbation-specific state dependence unestablished. Together the results define what action-related state information buys a delayed controller, where real circuits carry traces of it, and why demonstrating state-dependent perturbation effects requires controls that the field has not always applied.

## Introduction

A feedback controller that acts on delayed sensory measurements faces a fundamental problem: by the time the measurement arrives, the world and the animal have moved. Flying Drosophila stabilize and steer using visual input whose processing and transmission impose effective delays of tens to hundreds of milliseconds, yet perform saccade-scale maneuvers in which motor output changes within tens of milliseconds. Three classes of solution have been proposed. The animal may cancel the visual consequences of its own motion using an internal copy of the motor command (efference copy or corollary discharge); it may gate or down-weight the delayed visual pathway while generating fast, self-generated corrections; or it may rely on rapid non-visual mechanical feedback — for example from the antennae — that stabilizes the slower visual loop. Each class has empirical support in some form. Antennal feedback acts on a roughly 20-100 ms timescale and stabilizes optomotor behavior; motion-sensitive descending and descending-neighbor circuits carry signals related to self-motion; and behavioral suppression by visual context has been documented at multiple processing levels.

What has not been settled is the **computational necessity structure**: which piece of information is actually required to stabilize a delayed controller, what an estimator can and cannot replace, and how much of the corresponding signature real neural circuits show when examined with appropriately controlled analyses. These are distinct questions, and conflating them has produced both over-claims and premature dismissals.

We address them in three layers. First, in a delayed steering model ported from a published flight simulator, we exhaustively compare the solution classes above, including strong classical-control alternatives: forward-model (Smith-style) observers that see only delayed observations, instantaneous classical proportional-derivative control with matched gains and plant structure, and gating ablations at matched information. Second, we take the model's surviving claims to four public neural-behavioral datasets — a visual counter-evidence dataset with behavior and imaging at five cell-type levels, simultaneous HS-cell intracellular recordings and wing-beat signals during flight, and a walking-steering dataset with a genuine optogenetic intervention axis — and test what the data can and cannot say, with preregistration, negative controls, and animal-level statistics throughout. Third, we report the methodological result that emerged from the intervention analyses: tests of state-dependent perturbation responses are powerfully confounded by generic behavioral autocorrelation, and we quantify how much of the confound a residual pipeline removes and what remains.

Every empirical outcome is reported regardless of direction, including inconclusive and negative results; all confirmatory analyses were preregistered with frozen analysis files and hashes. The result is a map of necessity in the model, of signal in the data, and of the distance between them.
